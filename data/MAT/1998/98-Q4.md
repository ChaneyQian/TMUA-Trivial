## 题目
**(a)**

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ for each of the functions

$y = \sin(\ln x)$,

$y = x\sin(\ln x)$,

$y = x\cos(\ln x)$.

**(b)**

Sketch the following curves using the axes provided on the next page:

(i) $y = \ln x$, for $1 \leqslant x \leqslant \mathrm{e}^{\pi}$,

(ii) $y = \sin(\ln x)$, for $1 \leqslant x \leqslant \mathrm{e}^{\pi}$.

**(c)**

Evaluate

$$
\int_{1}^{\mathrm{e}^{\pi}} \sin(\ln x)\,dx.
$$

## 解析
**(a)** By the chain rule, with $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\ln x = \dfrac1x$,

$$y = \sin(\ln x) \implies \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \frac{\cos(\ln x)}{x}.$$

Then by the product rule,

$$y = x\sin(\ln x) \implies \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \sin(\ln x) + x \cdot \frac{\cos(\ln x)}{x} = \sin(\ln x) + \cos(\ln x),$$

$$y = x\cos(\ln x) \implies \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \cos(\ln x) - x \cdot \frac{\sin(\ln x)}{x} = \cos(\ln x) - \sin(\ln x).$$

**(b)(i)** On $1 \leqslant x \leqslant \mathrm{e}^{\pi}$ the curve $y = \ln x$ increases from $0$ to $\pi$, steeply at first and then flattening out.

**(b)(ii)** As $x$ runs over $\left[1, \mathrm{e}^{\pi}\right]$, $\ln x$ runs over $[0,\pi]$, so $y = \sin(\ln x)$ starts at $0$, rises to the maximum $1$ where $\ln x = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$, i.e. at $x = \mathrm{e}^{\pi/2} \approx 4.81$, and falls back to $0$ at $x = \mathrm{e}^{\pi} \approx 23.1$. It is a single positive arch, but skewed: the peak sits well to the left of the midpoint of the interval, and the descent is long and shallow.

**(c)** Adding the last two derivatives in (a),

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Bigl(x\sin(\ln x) - x\cos(\ln x)\Bigr) = \bigl(\sin + \cos\bigr)(\ln x) - \bigl(\cos - \sin\bigr)(\ln x) = 2\sin(\ln x).$$

Hence an antiderivative of $\sin(\ln x)$ is $\tfrac{x}{2}\bigl(\sin(\ln x) - \cos(\ln x)\bigr)$, and

$$\int_{1}^{\mathrm{e}^{\pi}} \sin(\ln x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \left[\frac{x}{2}\Bigl(\sin(\ln x) - \cos(\ln x)\Bigr)\right]_{1}^{\mathrm{e}^{\pi}}.$$

At $x = \mathrm{e}^{\pi}$ we have $\ln x = \pi$, so $\sin = 0$ and $\cos = -1$, giving $\tfrac{\mathrm{e}^{\pi}}{2}$. At $x=1$ we have $\ln x = 0$, so $\sin = 0$ and $\cos = 1$, giving $-\tfrac12$. Therefore

$$\int_{1}^{\mathrm{e}^{\pi}} \sin(\ln x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{\pi}}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{\pi}+1}{2}.$$
