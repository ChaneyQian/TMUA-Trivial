## 题目
The game of Hexaglide is played on a board with $3 \times 3$ squares; White and Black each have $3$ pieces, and they begin as shown in the first diagram. White moves first, and the players take turns to move one of their pieces forwards or backwards to an adjacent empty square. (Pieces never move sideways or diagonally, and they are never captured or removed from the board.)

![[Image/02-Q5-fig1.jpg]]

![[Image/02-Q5-fig2.jpg]]

![[Image/02-Q5-fig3.jpg]]

**(a)**

In a practice game, White plays without any Black pieces on the board, and, from his usual starting position, reaches the position shown in the second diagram. How many different sequences of moves end with this position if White makes (a) $6$ moves, (b) $7$ moves, (c) $8$ moves?

**(b)**

For parts (b) and (c) of this question, give ‘yes’ or ‘no’ answers; for both parts, you need not show your working or explain your answers.

In the real game, White and Black both play, and a player wins if he can trap his opponent's pieces so that they cannot move: the third diagram shows a win for White.

(a) Is it possible to reach the position shown in the third diagram?

(b) Is it possible to reach a position where Black has won?

(c) Can White play so as to ensure that either he wins or the game goes on forever?

(d) Can Black play so as to ensure that either he wins or the game goes on forever?

**(c)**

In an advanced version of the game, the board has $4 \times 4$ squares, and each player has $4$ pieces. What would be the answers to the four questions in part (b) in this case?

## 解析
Number the rows $1$ (top) to $3$ (bottom). Since pieces move only forwards and backwards, each piece stays in its own column for ever, and the black and white pieces of a column can never pass one another.

**(a)** With no black pieces on the board, the three columns are completely independent: each contains a single white piece which must travel from row $3$ to row $1$.

*Six moves.* Each white piece has to climb two rows, so it must make at least two moves, and $3 \times 2 = 6$ uses up the whole allowance. Hence every move is upwards and each column's pair of moves is completely determined; only the order in which the six moves are made varies. Choosing which two of the six slots belong to each column gives

$$\frac{6!}{2!\,2!\,2!} = 90 \text{ sequences.}$$

*Seven moves.* A piece that ends two rows above where it started makes a number of moves of the same parity as $2$, so each column takes an **even** number of moves. The total is then even, and $7$ is odd, so there are

$$0 \text{ sequences.}$$

*Eight moves.* The three column totals are even and at least $2$, and they sum to $8$, so they must be $4$, $2$, $2$ in some order. There are $3$ ways to choose the column that takes four moves. Within that column the piece makes four single-row moves from row $3$ to row $1$ without leaving the board, and writing out the positions in order shows there are exactly two such journeys:

$$3 \to 2 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 3 \to 2 \to 1 \to 2 \to 1 .$$

The other two columns have their two moves forced. Finally the three lists of moves (of lengths $4$, $2$, $2$) may be interleaved in $\dfrac{8!}{4!\,2!\,2!} = 420$ ways. Altogether

$$3 \times 2 \times 420 = 2520 \text{ sequences.}$$

**(b)** The answers are **(a) yes, (b) no, (c) yes, (d) no.**

The reason behind (a) and (b) is a parity count. Let $S$ be the sum, over the three columns, of the row numbers of the two pieces in that column. Initially every column contributes $1 + 3 = 4$, so $S = 12$. Each move changes one row number by $1$, so each move changes the parity of $S$; after $n$ moves $S \equiv 12 + n \pmod 2$. Also, it is White to play when $n$ is even and Black to play when $n$ is odd.

* In the third diagram every column has black on row $1$ and white on row $2$, so $S = 3\times 3 = 9$, which is odd. Hence $n$ is odd, i.e. it is Black's turn — exactly what a win for White requires. Nothing is contradicted, and the position is indeed reachable.
* For Black to win, every column would have to be white on row $3$ with black on row $2$, giving $S = 3 \times 5 = 15$, again odd, so again $n$ odd and Black to play. But Black winning requires it to be **White's** turn. This is impossible, so no position where Black has won can ever be reached.

For (c), White can actually force the win. Each column has exactly one empty square, at the top, in the middle, or at the bottom. A White move slides the gap between "middle" and "bottom"; a Black move slides it between "top" and "middle". So a column whose gap is at the bottom is dead for Black, and White is stuck only when every gap is at the top. All three gaps start in the middle, so White simply moves one gap from middle to bottom each turn. Black can only shuffle one of the remaining columns between top and middle, and whenever he returns a gap to the middle White immediately pushes it to the bottom. After at most three such White moves all three gaps are at the bottom and Black cannot move. Since White wins with correct play, Black cannot ensure a win or an endless game, so the answer to (d) is no.

**(c)** On the four-by-four board the four answers are reversed: **(a) no, (b) yes, (c) no, (d) yes.**

The same count settles the first two. Now each column contributes $1+4 = 5$ initially, so $S = 20$, which is even, and after $n$ moves $S \equiv 20 + n \pmod 2$.

* For White to win, every column would have to be black on row $1$, white on row $2$, giving $S = 4 \times 3 = 12$, which is even. So $n$ would be even and it would be White's turn, whereas a win for White needs Black to be on move. Impossible.
* For Black to win, every column would be black on row $3$, white on row $4$, giving $S = 4\times 7 = 28$, again even, so $n$ even and White to play — precisely what a win for Black requires. This is not excluded, and working through the game confirms that Black can force it.

Since Black wins with correct play, White cannot ensure a win or an endless game, so (c) is no and (d) is yes.
