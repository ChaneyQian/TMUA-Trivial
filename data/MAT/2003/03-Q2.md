## 题目
Let $k$ and $n$ be positive integers such that $n \geqslant 2k$ and
$$
\dfrac{(n - 2)!}{(n - 2k)!} = k! \, 2^{k-1}.
$$
(Recall that, for $r \geqslant 1$, $r!$ is the product $r \cdot (r - 1) \cdot (r - 2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1$ and that $0!$ is defined to equal $1$.)

**(a)**

Suppose that $k = 1$. What are the possible values of $n$?

**(b)**

Suppose that $k = 2$. Show that $(n - 2)(n - 3) = 4$. What are the possible values of $n$?

**(c)**

Suppose that $k = 3$. Show that it is impossible that $n \geqslant 7$.

**(d)**

Suppose that $k \geqslant 4$. Show that there are no possible values of $n$.

## 解析
The left-hand side is a product of consecutive integers:

$$\frac{(n-2)!}{(n-2k)!} = (n-2)(n-3)\cdots(n-2k+1),$$

a product of $2k-2$ consecutive integers, the largest being $n-2$ and the smallest $n-2k+1$. (When $k=1$ the product is empty and equals $1$.)

**(a)** If $k=1$ then $n-2k = n-2$, so the left-hand side is $\dfrac{(n-2)!}{(n-2)!} = 1$, while the right-hand side is $1!\,2^{0} = 1$. The equation therefore holds automatically, and the only constraints are that $n$ is a positive integer with $n \geqslant 2k = 2$. So

$$n = 2, 3, 4, 5, \ldots \quad \text{— every integer } n \geqslant 2.$$

**(b)** If $k = 2$ then $n - 2k = n-4$ and

$$\frac{(n-2)!}{(n-4)!} = (n-2)(n-3), \qquad k!\,2^{k-1} = 2! \times 2 = 4,$$

so $(n-2)(n-3) = 4$, as required. Expanding, $n^2 - 5n + 2 = 0$, whose roots $n = \tfrac{5 \pm \sqrt{17}}{2}$ are not integers. Checking directly is just as quick: the constraint is $n \geqslant 4$, and $(n-2)(n-3)$ takes the values $2, 6, 12, \ldots$ at $n = 4, 5, 6, \ldots$, increasing and never equal to $4$. Hence

$$\text{there are no possible values of } n.$$

**(c)** If $k=3$ then $n - 2k = n-6$ and the equation reads

$$(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)(n-5) = 3! \times 2^{2} = 24.$$

The left-hand side is increasing in $n$ for $n \geqslant 6$, and at $n = 7$ it already equals

$$5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 = 120 > 24.$$

So for every $n \geqslant 7$ the left-hand side exceeds $24$, and $n \geqslant 7$ is impossible. (The remaining case $n = 6$ does work, since $4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 24$.)

**(d)** Let $k \geqslant 4$. The left-hand side $(n-2)(n-3)\cdots(n-2k+1)$ is a product of $2k-2$ positive integers which increases with $n$, so over the permitted range $n \geqslant 2k$ its smallest value occurs at $n = 2k$, where it equals

$$(2k-2)!.$$

It is therefore enough to show $(2k-2)! > k!\,2^{k-1}$ for all $k \geqslant 4$.

For $k = 4$ this reads $6! = 720 > 4! \times 2^{3} = 192$, which is true. And in passing from $k$ to $k+1$ the left-hand side is multiplied by $(2k)(2k-1)$ while the right-hand side is multiplied by $2(k+1)$; since

$$(2k)(2k-1) > 2(k+1) \qquad \text{for } k \geqslant 2,$$

the inequality is preserved. By induction $(2k-2)! > k!\,2^{k-1}$ for every $k \geqslant 4$, so the left-hand side of the original equation is always strictly larger than the right-hand side and there are no possible values of $n$.
