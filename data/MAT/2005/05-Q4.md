## 题目
An $n \times n$ square array contains $0$s and $1$s. Such a square is given below with $n = 3$.
$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
0 & 0 & 1 \\
\hline
1 & 0 & 0 \\
\hline
1 & 1 & 0 \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

Two types of operation $C$ and $R$ may be performed on such an array.

- The first operation $C$ takes the first and second columns (on the left) and replaces them with a single column by comparing the two elements in each row as follows: if the two elements are the same then $C$ replaces them with a $1$, and if they differ $C$ replaces them with a $0$.
- The second operation $R$ takes the first and second rows (from the top) and replaces them with a single row by comparing the two elements in each column as follows: if the two elements are the same then $R$ replaces them with a $1$, and if they differ $R$ replaces them with a $0$.

By way of example, the effects of performing $R$ then $C$ on the square above are given below.

![[Image/05-Q4-fig1.jpg]]

**(a)**

If $R$ then $C$ are performed (in that order) on a $2 \times 2$ array then only a single number ($0$ or $1$) remains.

(i) Write down, in the grids provided, the eight $2 \times 2$ arrays which, when $R$ then $C$ are performed, produce a $1$.

(ii) By grouping your answers accordingly, show that if
$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|}
\hline
a & b \\
\hline
c & d \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$
is amongst your answers to part (i) then so is
$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|}
\hline
a & c \\
\hline
b & d \\
\hline
\end{array}.
$$
Explain why this means that doing $R$ then $C$ on a $2 \times 2$ array produces the same answer as doing $C$ first then $R$.

**(b)**

Consider now an $n \times n$ square array containing $0$s and $1$s, and the effects of performing $R$ then $C$ or $C$ then $R$ on the square.

(i) Explain why the effect on the right $n - 2$ columns is the same whether the order is $R$ then $C$ or $C$ then $R$. [This then also applies to the bottom $n - 2$ rows.]

(ii) Deduce that performing $R$ then $C$ on an $n \times n$ square produces the same result as performing $C$ then $R$.

## 解析
**(a)(i)** Apply the two operations to a general array $\begin{array}{|c|c|}\hline a & b\\\hline c& d\\\hline\end{array}$. The operation $R$ compares the two rows entry by entry, producing the single row

$$\bigl[\,a = c\,\bigr] \quad \bigl[\,b = d\,\bigr],$$

writing $[\,\cdot\,]$ for $1$ if the statement is true and $0$ if it is false. Then $C$ compares those two entries, so the final number is $1$ exactly when the statements "$a=c$" and "$b=d$" are either both true or both false.

If $a = c$ and $b = d$ then $a+b+c+d = 2a+2b$ is even; if $a \neq c$ and $b \neq d$ then $a+c = 1$ and $b+d=1$, so the sum is $2$, again even. Conversely, an even sum forces $a+c$ and $b+d$ to have the same parity, so the two statements agree. Hence

$$\text{the result is } 1 \iff a+b+c+d \text{ is even,}$$

and the eight arrays are

$$\begin{array}{cc} 0&0\\0&0 \end{array} \quad \begin{array}{cc} 1&1\\0&0 \end{array} \quad \begin{array}{cc} 1&0\\1&0 \end{array} \quad \begin{array}{cc} 0&0\\1&1 \end{array} \quad \begin{array}{cc} 0&1\\0&1 \end{array} \quad \begin{array}{cc} 1&0\\0&1 \end{array} \quad \begin{array}{cc} 0&1\\1&0 \end{array} \quad \begin{array}{cc} 1&1\\1&1 \end{array}$$

**(a)(ii)** Group them as: the two arrays with all entries equal ($0000$ and $1111$), the two "diagonal" arrays $\begin{array}{cc}1&0\\0&1\end{array}$ and $\begin{array}{cc}0&1\\1&0\end{array}$, and the two pairs

$$\begin{array}{cc} 1&1\\0&0 \end{array} \leftrightarrow \begin{array}{cc} 1&0\\1&0 \end{array}, \qquad \begin{array}{cc} 0&0\\1&1 \end{array} \leftrightarrow \begin{array}{cc} 0&1\\0&1 \end{array}.$$

Interchanging $b$ and $c$ — which is what the question asks about — leaves the first four arrays unchanged and swaps the members of each of the last two pairs. So the list of eight is unchanged as a whole: whenever $\begin{array}{cc}a&b\\c&d\end{array}$ is on it, so is $\begin{array}{cc}a&c\\b&d\end{array}$. (This is clear from the criterion just proved: interchanging $b$ and $c$ does not change $a+b+c+d$.)

Now, interchanging $b$ and $c$ is exactly transposing the array, which swaps the roles of rows and columns. Hence performing $C$ then $R$ on an array gives the same number as performing $R$ then $C$ on its transpose. Since the collection of arrays yielding $1$ under "$R$ then $C$" is unchanged by transposition, an array yields $1$ under "$R$ then $C$" if and only if it yields $1$ under "$C$ then $R$". As both procedures produce a single $0$ or $1$, they always produce the same answer.

**(b)(i)** The operation $C$ alters only the first two columns, replacing them by one new column and leaving the other $n-2$ columns exactly as they were. The operation $R$ alters only the first two rows, merging them within every column.

So look at the right-hand $n-2$ columns. Performing $R$ then $C$: $R$ merges their top two entries and $C$ leaves them alone. Performing $C$ then $R$: $C$ leaves them alone and then $R$ merges their top two entries. The outcome is the same either way. By the identical argument with rows and columns interchanged, the bottom $n-2$ rows also come out the same.

**(b)(ii)** After either order of operations the array is $(n-1)\times(n-1)$. Every entry of it lies in the right-hand $n-2$ columns, or in the bottom $n-2$ rows, or is the single entry in the top-left corner. The first two kinds agree by (i).

The top-left entry is produced by merging the top two rows and then the left two columns (in one order or the other) of the top-left $2\times2$ block

$$\begin{array}{|c|c|}\hline a_{11} & a_{12}\\\hline a_{21} & a_{22}\\\hline\end{array},$$

no other entries being involved. By part (a) that single entry is the same whichever order is used. Hence every entry agrees, and performing $R$ then $C$ on an $n \times n$ square gives the same result as performing $C$ then $R$.
