## 题目
$g(x) = f(x)\left[f(x) + f(-x)\right]$. Which of the following is true?

	I. $f(x)$ is even $\implies g(x)$ is even
	II. $g(x)$ is odd $\implies f(x)$ is odd
	III. $g(x) = 0$ for all $x$ $\implies f(x)$ is odd

## 答案
H

## 解析
**回忆答案:** I, II and III

The whole question comes out of one identity. Replacing $x$ by $-x$ in the definition gives $g(-x) = f(-x)\left[f(-x) + f(x)\right]$, so adding,

$$g(x) + g(-x) = \left[f(x) + f(-x)\right]^{2} . \qquad (\star)$$

**I is true.** If $f$ is even then $f(-x) = f(x)$, so $g(x) = f(x)\left[f(x) + f(x)\right] = 2f(x)^{2}$. Hence $g(-x) = 2f(-x)^{2} = 2f(x)^{2} = g(x)$, and $g$ is even. (Squaring destroys the sign information, which is why the parity of $g$ here does not follow the "even $\times$ even" slogan but is simply even.)

**II is true.** If $g$ is odd then $g(-x) = -g(x)$ for all $x$, so the left-hand side of $(\star)$ is $0$. Thus $\left[f(x) + f(-x)\right]^{2} = 0$ for every $x$, and since this is a real square, $f(-x) = -f(x)$ for every $x$: $f$ is odd.

**III is true.** If $g(x) = 0$ for all $x$ then again the left-hand side of $(\star)$ is $0$, so exactly as in II we get $f(-x) = -f(x)$ for all $x$ and $f$ is odd. (It is tempting to argue only from $f(x)\left[f(x) + f(-x)\right] = 0$ that at each $x$ either $f(x) = 0$ or $f(-x) = -f(x)$, and to hope for a counterexample in which $f$ vanishes somewhere without being odd; but $(\star)$ closes that gap, because at a point with $f(x_0) = 0$ the same equation at $-x_0$ forces $f(-x_0) = 0$ as well.)

All three statements hold.

The answer is H.
